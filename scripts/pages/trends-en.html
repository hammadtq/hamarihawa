Every burning season since 2019

Each line is one season, 1 October to 30 November, when farmers burn rice stubble before sowing wheat. The red line is this year. To compare years fairly, these charts count only what the NOAA-20 satellite saw, because it has flown every season since 2019. (They counted Suomi NPP until NASA stopped delivering its data on 2 November 2026.) Newer satellites see more fires, so adding them would make recent years look worse than they are.

Season totals

The dark part of each bar is 1 October up to the latest full day of this season, so you can compare this year with the same dates in earlier years. The light part is the rest of that season.

Where the burning moves

Each bubble is a district, sized by the fire spots all satellites saw there that day. Pick a year and press play to watch a season unfold. Most of the burning is in the two Punjabs: Sangrur, Bathinda, Firozpur and Moga on the Indian side, and Jhang, Okara, Hafizabad, Gujranwala and Nankana Sahib on the Pakistani side.

Day and night

NOAA-20 crosses the region around 1:30 in the afternoon and 1:30 at night. Each bar shows what share of a season's fires it caught on the night pass. That share has grown from about 3% in 2019 to about 8% in 2025. A fire lit at 4 pm that dies down before 1:30 am is missed by both passes, and that is now common: iFOREST, using a satellite that looks every 15 minutes, found over 90% of large farm fires in Indian Punjab in 2024 and 2025 started after 3 pm, against 3% in 2021. So these counts are a floor, not a total, and the fall since 2021 is partly fires the satellites missed. Burnt-area maps show Indian Punjab's burning fell by about a third from 2022 to 2025, not the 90% the counts suggest. Our Two Punjabs report looks at this side by side, and finds no sign of the same shift in Pakistani Punjab.

How this is counted

  • Area: Northern plains (the default) counts 25–35.5°N, 66–88.5°E, from Peshawar and the edge of Karachi to Patna, where the stubble is burnt; the archive has covered it since 2019. Whole countries counts all of Pakistan and India, which brings in coal fields, forest fires and shifting cultivation in the east and south. Every season was rebuilt for both countries in October 2026, so both choices compare all years.
  • Fire spots: places hot enough for the satellite to notice, in 375 m squares. The same spot seen by several satellites counts once.
  • Cloud and smoke hide fires, so single days swing. The season lines use a 7-day average.
  • Missing days: NOAA-20 returned almost no data on 1 October 2023. That day is left out of the lines and counts as zero in the totals.
  • Days run on Pakistan time, so the 1:30 am pass belongs to the date it happened on.
  • Sources: NASA FIRMS. Past seasons are from NASA's final processing; this season is from its near-real-time feed, which can differ by a few percent.
