Report · 11 Nov 2026

Delhi's air is polluted all year. Autumn crop fires add to it

By Hammad Tariq

Delhi's fine-particle pollution (PM2.5) averaged {{pm_12_months.mean}} µg/m³ over the {{pm_12_months.days_with_value}} days with data in the 12 months to October 2026, at government monitors in and around the city. India's standard for a year is {{standards.india_annual}}. The pollution is there in every season. In December and January, after the main rice-residue burning season, it is about as high as in the burning weeks. Each October and November, crop fires burn in Punjab and Haryana, to the north-west of the city. In the available late-October and early-November records, Delhi's PM2.5 was higher in the groups of days with some or many fire detections upwind than in the group with few. The size of the difference varied by period and by how the data were selected. This report cannot say what percentage of Delhi's pollution comes from the fires.

Polluted in every season

We used the readings of every government reference monitor within 25 km of central Delhi, as republished by OpenAQ [1]: stations run by the Delhi Pollution Control Committee, the India Meteorological Department, the Central Pollution Control Board and the pollution boards of neighbouring Uttar Pradesh and Haryana. Over the past year a typical day had {{pm_series.typical_stations_last_12_months}} of them reporting. For each day we took the median across stations, and for each month the average of those daily values.

The monthly average was {{pm_monthly_12.2025-11.mean}} µg/m³ in November 2025 and {{pm_monthly_12.2025-12.mean}} in December. It was lowest in the monsoon: {{pm_monthly_12.2026-07.mean}} in July 2026, {{pm_monthly_12.2026-08.mean}} in August and {{pm_monthly_12.2026-09.mean}} in September. India's national standards for PM2.5 are {{standards.india_24h}} µg/m³ over 24 hours and {{standards.india_annual}} over a year [2]. The World Health Organization's guideline values are {{standards.who_24h}} and {{standards.who_annual}} [3].

Of the {{pm_12_months.calendar_days}} days from November 2025 to October 2026, {{pm_12_months.days_with_value}} have a city value. Their average was {{pm_12_months.mean}}, which is {{pm_12_months.times_annual_standard}} times the annual standard. It is not an official annual average, and the missing days are not spread evenly: January has {{pm_monthly_12.2026-01.days}} of {{pm_monthly_12.2026-01.days_in_month}} days, February {{pm_monthly_12.2026-02.days}} of {{pm_monthly_12.2026-02.days_in_month}} and October {{pm_monthly_12.2026-10.days}} of {{pm_monthly_12.2026-10.days_in_month}}. On {{pm_12_months.days_over_60}} of the {{pm_12_months.days_with_value}} days the city value was above {{standards.india_24h}}, and on {{pm_12_months.days_over_15}} it was above the WHO's 24-hour value of {{standards.who_24h}}.

The {{pm_12_months.mean}} is a figure for the whole year, burning weeks included. It is not Delhi's level without fires. From February to September 2026 the average was {{pm_outside_autumn.feb_sep}} ({{pm_outside_autumn.feb_sep_days}} days). Those months include the wheat harvest of April and May, when crop residue is also burnt [14]. This report does not examine it. Only in the four monsoon months, June to September, was the average close to the annual standard, at {{pm_outside_autumn.jun_sep}}.

India's environment ministry lists the sources behind the region's pollution as vehicles, industry, construction and road dust, biomass burning and waste burning, among others. It says stubble burning and firecrackers add to them at times, and that paddy stubble burning affects the region's air particularly in October and November [13]. This report does not measure any of these sources.

December and January are about as polluted as the burning weeks. By December the main rice-residue burning season is over. Only three seasons have enough days of data in both periods to compare.

What changes in October and November

The air mixes less. Pollution spreads upward into a layer of air whose depth changes with the season. Over central Delhi that layer averaged {{weather_climatology.4.blh}} m in April and {{weather_climatology.5.blh}} m in May, across all hours of the day, in the ERA5 weather reanalysis for 2016–2025 [4]. It averaged {{weather_climatology.10.blh}} m in October, {{weather_climatology.11.blh}} m in November and {{weather_climatology.12.blh}} m in December. At night, from October to December, it averaged {{weather_climatology.night_oct_dec_min}} to {{weather_climatology.night_oct_dec_max}} m. The same emissions go into much less air. These are ten-year averages from a weather model, not measurements for 2025–26.

The wind turns to come from the north-west. In the monsoon months, the wind 100 m above Delhi blows from the west-to-north quarter {{weather_climatology.nw_monsoon_min}}% to {{weather_climatology.nw_monsoon_max}}% of the time. In October that is {{weather_climatology.10.from_nw_pct}}%, in November {{weather_climatology.11.from_nw_pct}}% and in December {{weather_climatology.12.from_nw_pct}}%. That is the direction of Haryana and Punjab. It is also the usual winter wind of north India, so a north-west wind on a given day is not evidence of smoke.

The fields burn. After the rice harvest, many farmers in Punjab and Haryana burn crop residue to clear fields for wheat. NASA's MODIS satellite instruments [5] recorded between {{fires_range.min_2016_2021|,}} and {{fires_range.max_2016_2021|,}} fire detections in the two states in each October–November from 2016 to 2021. Most detections are far from Delhi. In each year from 2016 to 2025, the median Punjab detection was {{fire_distance.punjab_km_min}} to {{fire_distance.punjab_km_max}} km from the city centre and the median Haryana detection {{fire_distance.haryana_km_min}} to {{fire_distance.haryana_km_max}} km. Only {{fire_distance.within_100km_pct}}% of the detections on the Indian side of our study area were within 100 km of Delhi.

Fires upwind and Delhi's air

What we compared. Smoke reaches Delhi only if the wind carries it there. So for each day from 15 October to 30 November we traced the air arriving over central Delhi back 48 hours along the wind, four times a day. We then counted the MODIS fire detections within 25 km of that path that were recorded between 24 hours before the air passed and 3 hours after. We call this count the estimated upstream fire-detection index. It is a rough indicator. It does not show that the air crossed a burning field, and it misses fires the satellites did not see.

Of the detections counted this way, {{path_index_days.where_pct.indian_punjab}}% were in Indian Punjab, {{path_index_days.where_pct.haryana}}% in Haryana and {{path_index_days.where_pct.pakistan}}% in Pakistan. There are {{path_index_days.days}} days with both an index and a Delhi value. Most are from three seasons: {{path_index_days.by_year.2019}} days in 2019, {{path_index_days.by_year.2021}} in 2021 and {{path_index_days.by_year.2025}} in 2025. There are {{path_index_days.by_year.2018}} in 2018, {{path_index_days.by_year.2026}} in 2026 and {{path_index_days.days_in_2016_2017_2020}} in 2016, 2017 and 2020 together. We divided the days into thirds by the index: few detections ({{path_index_days.few_max}} or fewer), some, and many (more than {{path_index_days.many_above}}).

In the second half of October, Delhi's median PM2.5 was {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.15-31 Oct.few.median}} µg/m³ on days in the few category, {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.15-31 Oct.some.median}} on days in the some category and {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.15-31 Oct.many.median}} on days in the many category. From 1 to 15 November it was {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.1-15 Nov.few.median}}, {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.1-15 Nov.some.median}} and {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.1-15 Nov.many.median}}, though only {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.1-15 Nov.few.days}} days in that fortnight were in the few category. From 16 to 30 November there is no such pattern: {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.16-30 Nov.few.median}}, {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.16-30 Nov.some.median}} and {{pm_by_path_index.pooled_thirds.16-30 Nov.many.median}}.

So in late October and early November the some and many groups were both above the few group. It is not a steady step: in late October the many group was below the some group.

A check on the older data. The older records can be read on two hourly grids (see How we did this). We used one. The other gives more days: {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.days_with_index_alternative}} instead of {{path_index_days.days}}. For this larger sample we recalculated the thirds: few means {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.few_max}} or fewer detections and many means more than {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.many_above}}. Keeping the original boundaries gives the same qualitative conclusion. With the recalculated thirds, the three groups are {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.15-31 Oct.few.median}}, {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.15-31 Oct.some.median}} and {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.15-31 Oct.many.median}} in late October and {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.1-15 Nov.few.median}}, {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.1-15 Nov.some.median}} and {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.1-15 Nov.many.median}} in early November, and late November again shows no pattern ({{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.16-30 Nov.few.median}}, {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.16-30 Nov.some.median}} and {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.median_pm.16-30 Nov.many.median}}). On this grid the many group is the highest in both periods, and in early November the few and some groups are about equal. On either grid the many group is above the few group in late October and early November. The sizes of the gaps change.

A comparison with winter days. October and November are also when the air over Delhi mixes less, so the pattern above could be partly weather. As a rough check, we paired each day of the peak burning weeks, 25 October to 20 November, with December–January days of the same season that had similar ventilation (mixing height multiplied by wind speed). This matches one weather measure only. It does not match temperature, humidity, rain or wind direction. The winter days are not Delhi without fires.

Only {{matched_comparison.days}} days can be paired this way: {{matched_comparison.by_year.2019}} in 2019, {{matched_comparison.by_year.2021}} in 2021 and {{matched_comparison.by_year.2025}} in 2025. They averaged {{matched_comparison.mean_pm}} µg/m³, and their paired winter days averaged {{matched_comparison.matched_winter}}.

On the {{matched_comparison.few.days}} days in the few category, Delhi averaged {{matched_comparison.few.mean_pm}}, against {{matched_comparison.few.matched_winter}} on the paired winter days. On the {{matched_comparison.some.days}} days in the some category it averaged {{matched_comparison.some.mean_pm}}, against {{matched_comparison.some.matched_winter}}. On the {{matched_comparison.many.days}} days in the many category it averaged {{matched_comparison.many.mean_pm}}, against {{matched_comparison.many.matched_winter}}: {{matched_comparison.many.difference}} higher.

The seasons are uneven. The {{matched_comparison.few.days}} few-category days come from 2019 and 2025 only; 2021 has none. In 2021 the {{matched_comparison.by_season.2021.many_days}} many-category days were {{matched_comparison.by_season.2021.excess_many}} above their paired winter days and the other days were {{matched_comparison.by_season.2021.excess_not_many|abs}} below. In 2025 the {{matched_comparison.by_season.2025.many_days}} many-category days were {{matched_comparison.by_season.2025.excess_many}} above and the others {{matched_comparison.by_season.2025.excess_not_many}} above. In 2019 only {{matched_comparison.by_season.2019.many_days}} of the {{matched_comparison.by_season.2019.days}} days was in the many category.

These differences from winter depend on which data are used. On the other hourly grid there are {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.matched_days_alternative}} paired days in {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.seasons_alternative}} seasons. The few-category days are then {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.difference_from_winter.few.mean}} above their paired winter days, not {{matched_comparison.few.difference}}, the some-category days {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.difference_from_winter.some.mean}} above and the many-category days {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.thirds_re_estimated.difference_from_winter.many.mean}} above. The order of the three groups holds. The numbers do not.

Why we give no percentage. These comparisons do not establish the percentage of Delhi's pollution that comes from crop fires. Days with few detections may still contain smoke, the groups differ by year and in other conditions, and winter days matched on one ventilation measure are not an equivalent no-fire baseline. We therefore report the concentrations we observed and how they are associated.

A published estimate. India's SAFAR forecasting system provided daily model estimates of crop-fire smoke's share of Delhi's PM2.5 for the 2021 season. The Centre for Science and Environment analysed them for {{published_cse_safar.days}} days from 10 October to 30 November 2021 [6]. The daily share ranged from {{published_cse_safar.daily_share_min_pct}}% to {{published_cse_safar.daily_share_max_pct}}%, with its peak on 7 November 2021, and was above 40% on {{published_cse_safar.days_above_40pct}} days. That is a model's estimate for one season. We have not checked it, and our comparison does not test it.

Two other checks were inconclusive. We looked at carbon monoxide at Delhi's monitors and at satellite readings of haze over the city. Neither separated the many-category days from the rest. The carbon monoxide data are usable only for 2025 and 2026, and haze seen from space includes everything in the air column. These checks neither support nor contradict the pattern above. Both are in the data file.

Fire counts have fallen. Burning may have fallen less

MODIS recorded {{fires_by_year.2021.punjab_haryana|,}} detections in Punjab and Haryana in October–November 2021 and {{fires_by_year.2025.punjab_haryana|,}} in the same months of 2025. The VIIRS instrument on the NOAA-20 satellite recorded {{fires_second_sensor.2021|,}} between 1 October and 10 November 2021 and {{fires_second_sensor.2025|,}} in the same weeks of 2025 [7]. This year, to 10 November, MODIS has recorded {{fires_by_year.2026.punjab_haryana|,}} in early, unreviewed data.

These counts do not measure how much was burnt, and the two instruments agreeing does not settle it. These instruments sample fires at particular times of day. Changes in burning time and in satellite orbits mean that the same fraction of fires is not observed every year.

  • Burning has moved later in the day. A 2026 study by Hiren Jethva, using a geostationary satellite that observes through the afternoon, found that peak burning in the region moved from about 1–2 pm to 4–5 pm. Over Punjab, the hotspots that satellite detected were {{published_jethva_2026.geostationary_punjab_2024_vs_2021_pct|abs}}% fewer in 2024 than in 2021 and {{published_jethva_2026.geostationary_punjab_2025_vs_2024_pct}}% more in 2025 than in 2024 [8]. Its pixels are about {{published_jethva_2026.geostationary_pixel_km}} km wide. These are detected hotspots, not a count of every field fire.
  • Burnt area fell less than fire counts. The research group iFOREST reports that more than {{published_iforest.punjab_large_fires_after_3pm_2024_2025_pct_more_than}}% of large farm fires in Punjab in 2024 and 2025 were detected after 3 pm, against {{published_iforest.punjab_large_fires_after_3pm_2021_pct}}% in 2021. It reports that Punjab's burnt area fell from {{published_iforest.punjab_burnt_area_km2_2022_peak|,}} km² in 2022 to about {{published_iforest.punjab_burnt_area_km2_2025_about|,}} km² in 2025, a fall of {{published_iforest.punjab_burnt_area_change_pct|abs}}%, while satellite fire counts fell {{published_iforest.active_fire_counts_change_2021_to_2025_pct.punjab|abs}}% from 2021 [9]. The two falls are measured from different years, so they are not a like-for-like pair.
  • The MODIS satellites have drifted. NASA says the orbits of Terra and Aqua have drifted, changing the time of day they pass and widening gaps between their scans, with significant effects from late 2024 [10]. In our data, Terra's detections in the two states fell from {{fires_by_year.2021.terra|,}} in 2021 to {{fires_by_year.2025.terra}} in 2025, and Aqua's from {{fires_by_year.2021.aqua|,}} to {{fires_by_year.2025.aqua|,}}. The study above reports that Aqua's average pass over the region moved from {{published_jethva_2026.aqua_mean_overpass_local_time.2021}} in 2021 to {{published_jethva_2026.aqua_mean_overpass_local_time.2025}} in 2025 [8].

We have not independently reproduced the two studies' analyses. Because of these three things, we do not use the yearly counts to say how much burning has fallen, and we do not infer a trend or a cause from year-to-year comparisons of Delhi's air. Delhi's November average was {{fires_range.delhi_nov_2021}} µg/m³ in 2021 and {{fires_range.delhi_nov_2025}} in 2025, with {{fires_range.delhi_nov_2021_days}} days of data in 2021 and {{fires_range.delhi_nov_2025_days}} in 2025. Weather differs between years, and two Novembers cannot show a trend.

What this report can't tell you

  • What share of Delhi's pollution comes from crop fires. A credible percentage requires a validated attribution method, such as chemical analysis of the particles or an air-quality model checked against measurements. These comparisons do not establish one.
  • What Delhi's air would be without the fires. Neither the yearly average nor the winter level is that.
  • Where the air really went. Our paths follow the ERA5 wind 100 m above the ground. They ignore vertical motion and the spreading of smoke, and they stop at 48 hours. We did not compare them with an established tool such as HYSPLIT.
  • How much was really burnt. Satellite detections miss fires under cloud or smoke and fires lit outside the times the satellites pass. That affects the yearly counts and the index for each day. The extent of this bias varies between years and instruments.
  • Fireworks. Diwali falls in or close to the burning weeks, and fireworks are let off on other days too. We cannot separate them from fire smoke.
  • How much the choice of older data matters. On both hourly grids the many group is above the few group in late October and early November. The sizes of the differences, and the differences from winter, change with the grid.
  • Most years. Our Delhi record has no eligible day from 17 October 2022 to 18 February 2025, and few in 2016, 2017, 2018 and 2020. The comparison rests mainly on 2019, 2021 and 2025. We did not fill the gaps from another source.
  • Whether the monitors are right. We used the official readings as republished and did not check any station against another instrument. The set of stations changed over the years: a typical November day had {{pm_series.typical_stations_in_november.2017}} stations in 2017, {{pm_series.typical_stations_in_november.2019}} in 2019, {{pm_series.typical_stations_in_november.2021}} in 2021 and {{pm_series.typical_stations_in_november.2025}} in 2025.

How we did this

PM2.5. OpenAQ records for the PM2.5 sensors of government monitors within 25 km of 28.61°N 77.21°E, retrieved on 11 November 2026. From 2025 we used OpenAQ's daily records. For 1 October to 31 January of the seasons 2016–17 to 2022–23 we used its hourly records, because the daily counts for those years can include overlapping hourly aggregates. We selected one consistent grid of non-overlapping hourly aggregates, those ending on the hour in India time, and required at least 18 valid aggregates on that grid. This conservative restriction excludes some available offset-hour observations. From 2025 a station-day needs at least 18 of its 24 hours. In both, its average must be above 0 and no higher than 1,500 µg/m³. The city value is the median across stations, on days with at least five. That gives {{pm_series.days}} days: {{pm_series.days_from_hourly_records_2016_2022}} from the hourly records and {{pm_series.days_from_daily_records_2025_2026}} from 2025 onward. Monthly and seasonal figures are averages of the daily values. On the other grid, of aggregates ending on the half hour, the historical records give {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.historical_city_days_alternative}} days instead of {{sensitivity_hourly_grid.historical_city_days_main}}. That grid shifts the older days by half an hour, so we used it only as a check.

Fires. NASA FIRMS MODIS detections (Terra and Aqua), 1 October to 30 November, standard product to 2025 and near-real-time data for 2026 (to 10 November), confidence 30 or more, presumed vegetation fires, in the area 73.0–79.5°E, 27.0–32.6°N. Each detection keeps its time of acquisition in UTC and is dated in India time. It is assigned to a country and an Indian state by boundary polygons [12].

Weather. ERA5 hourly mixing height and wind at the grid point nearest central Delhi, from the Open-Meteo archive, in India time. Daily ventilation is the mixing height multiplied by the 100 m wind speed, averaged over the same calendar day as the PM2.5 value.

Upstream fire-detection index. ERA5 100 m wind on a half-degree grid over 27.5–33°N, 72.5–78.5°E, inside a one-degree grid over 24–35°N, 69–83°E. From central Delhi at 03:00, 09:00, 15:00 and 21:00 India time, each path steps back 48 hours in 15-minute steps. A detection counts if it is within 25 km of an hourly point on a path and was acquired from 24 hours before to 3 hours after the air was at that point. The day's index is the number of distinct detections over the four paths. A day is used only if no path stopped for lack of wind data; {{path_index_days.days_with_four_complete_paths}} of the {{path_index_days.days}} days have four full 48-hour paths, and on the rest a path left the wind grid early. The wind data end on 5 November 2026. The last day with both an index and a Delhi value is 2 November 2026.

Winter comparison. Each day from 25 October to 20 November is paired with up to five December–January days of the same season (at least three) whose ventilation is within a factor of 1.25 of its own. The {{matched_comparison.control_reuse.target_days}} days use {{matched_comparison.control_reuse.distinct_control_days}} different winter days, {{matched_comparison.control_reuse.control_days_used_more_than_once}} of them more than once. Every pair is listed with the data.

The data behind every chart and table, and the scripts that make them, are in the site's repository under scripts/reports/delhi-smog-fires/.
