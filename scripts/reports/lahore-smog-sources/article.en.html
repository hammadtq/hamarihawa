Lahore's smog, source by source: what satellites can see, and what they can't

By Hammad Tariq

Lahore's air reached an AQI of 448 to over 500 on 1–2 November. Traffic, factories, brick kilns and power plants run all year, so they set the city's everyday level. What arrives at the end of October is crop burning on the city's edge, and still, cold air that holds the smoke down. We looked at every source satellites can measure. Lahore's nitrogen dioxide, the gas from engines and furnaces, stays at about twice the level of the farmland around it, with no clear change since 2023. Crop fires within 40 km of the city ran at hundreds a week. Lahore's two big dumps showed no open fire from space; their problem is methane. Dust raised coarse particles on a handful of days, not the fine smoke behind an AQI of 500.

What changes when the smog arrives

Lahore was above Pakistan's legal limit for PM2.5 on every day of October, at an average of 100 µg/m³ across 26 ground monitors, according to the Pakistan Air Quality Initiative (PAQI). On 1 and 2 November the city's AQI was reported at 448 to over 500.

Most of Lahore's sources don't switch on in November. Vehicles, industry, kilns and power plants burn fuel all year. Two things change at the end of October. The rice harvest ends and the stubble is burnt, and the air turns cold and still, so smoke stays near the ground instead of mixing upwards. October 2026 and October 2025 had Lahore's slowest winds in eight years: an average of 4.9 and 4.4 m/s, against 6.6–7.8 m/s in 2019–2024 (ERA5 weather reanalysis). PAQI estimates that this year's weather alone cut Lahore's October PM2.5 by about 19% compared with last year; weather can make the same emissions much better or much worse.

In that view, the satellites counted 135 fire spots on 1 October and 1,538 on 1 November. As the dots multiply, the grey haze over the plains thickens.

Crop fires on the city's edge

From 15 October to 1 November, satellites recorded 781 fire spots on farmland within 40 km of central Lahore: about as far as Muridke or Raiwind. The biggest share was across the Ravi in Sheikhupura district (322), then Tarn Taran (149) and Amritsar (106) on the Indian side, Nankana Sahib (77), Lahore district itself (62) and Kasur (50). Within 100 km there were 7,130.

Over the same days, only 72 spots within 40 km were at kilns, factories, towns or dumps. Satellites see open field fires well; they see enclosed burning, in kilns, furnaces and engines, poorly or not at all. So these counts show where the seasonal smoke comes from, not each source's share of Lahore's air.

Traffic and industry: nitrogen dioxide

Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) comes from fuel burnt at high temperature: vehicle engines, power plants and furnaces. The European Sentinel-5P satellite measures it every day. We averaged the same 18 days, 15 October to 1 November, in each year from 2023, the first year NASA's map service carries it.

Every year, Lahore's NO₂ is about 1.6 to 2 times the level over farmland to its south-west. It doesn't show a clear rise or fall: 2026 is about 10% below 2023, which is within what weather alone can do, and 2024 and 2025 were processed differently by NASA, so they compare less cleanly. The highest levels sit over the city core, with a second patch in some years over the north-west, along the Ravi and Sheikhupura Road, where several power plants and industrial estates are. On our live map for 18–31 October, Lahore had two of Pakistan's top four NO₂ hotspots, after Rawalpindi and Karachi: one in the north-west near Shahdara and one in the south-centre.

What NO₂ can't do: it can't separate a motorcycle from a truck, a bus or a factory chimney, and it says nothing about fuel quality. Motorcycles give off mostly carbon monoxide, unburnt fuel and fine particles rather than NO₂. Sulphur in diesel and furnace oil shows up as SO₂, which satellites measure too noisily to pin on one city.

Brick kilns and power plants

Research datasets that map kilns from satellite images place 898 brick kilns within 50 km of central Lahore on the Pakistani side, 197 of them within 25 km. Kilns burn coal, wood and waste inside a chimney, so fire satellites rarely see them: only 20 detections within 40 km were at kilns from 15 October to 1 November. Whether a kiln is running or shut, as Punjab's smog orders require at times, can't be read from these images.

Seven oil- and gas-fired power plants sit within 50 km, mostly along Sheikhupura Road to the north-west, the largest being the 1,231 MW Bhikki plant. OpenStreetMap shows 215 industrial areas within 50 km.

Lahore's dumps: methane, not smoke

In March 2025, BBC Urdu reported how Mehmood Booti, a 14-million-tonne dump on Lahore's Ring Road closed in 2016, was being capped with soil, with pipes to vent its methane. The gas would be sold to nearby industry if there was enough of it, or burnt off if not. Since then, most of the city's rubbish, about 5,000 tonnes a day, goes to Lakhodair, a few kilometres to the north-east.

From space, neither dump is burning in the open. NASA's fire satellites caught a handful of fires on the Mehmood Booti mound in the 2020–2022 seasons and none since. At Lakhodair, we checked 86 clear Sentinel-2 images since October 2024 and found no large fire on the dump's surface; the few hot readings were clouds or a pixel or two. Fire detections near Lakhodair fall on neighbouring sites, not on the dump.

The dumps' problem is methane. Carbon Mapper, which measures methane from aircraft and satellites, recorded 1,559 kg an hour coming off Lakhodair, with a plume seen in 19 passes and on 92% of the times it looked, from June 2023 to March 2026. It has no plume recorded for Mehmood Booti. Methane is a powerful warming gas and helps form ozone, but it isn't the fine smoke behind Lahore's AQI. Mehmood Booti's vents still let its methane out unburnt, as BBC reported.

Dust

The Copernicus air model, which has a separate dust figure, shows nine dusty days at Lahore in October, when its modelled dust reached 50 µg/m³ or more: 1–3, 7–11 and 30 October. Dust made up about 43% of the model's PM10, the coarser particles, over the month. But PM2.5 was no higher on dusty days than on other days (65 against 70 µg/m³ in the model). Dust matters for PM10; it isn't what sends Lahore's AQI to 500. The model reads Lahore well below ground monitors, so these are its proportions, not measured levels. Construction dust is too local for it to see.

What satellites can't tell you

Satellites can't give each source's share of Lahore's air. Two published estimates disagree sharply. The Urban Unit's 2023 emission inventory for Lahore put transport at about 83% of total emissions, while noting the vehicle figure may be an overestimate. PAQI's national study, reported by Dawn in December 2025, gave transport 35%, heavy industry 28% and brick kilns 17%; Punjab's Environment Protection Department rejected it. Both are calculations from activity data, not measurements.

What the satellite record does show is narrower but solid: Lahore's traffic-and-industry gas is high every year and isn't falling; the fires that arrive in late October are on farmland within reach of the city, not at its dumps; and dust is a coarse-particle problem on a few days. The rest, such as vehicle types, fuel quality, construction and street burning of rubbish, needs measurements on the ground.

How we did this

  • Fires: NASA FIRMS, all VIIRS and MODIS satellites, from HamariHawa's daily archive. "Crop" is a seasonal label for fires on farmland away from known kilns, dumps, industry and towns. Distances are from central Lahore (31.55 N, 74.34 E).
  • Time-lapse: NASA Worldview VIIRS (NOAA-20) true-colour images, one a day, with that day's fire spots from all satellites.
  • NO₂: Copernicus Sentinel-5P TROPOMI tropospheric NO₂ via NASA GIBS, read back from published colour bands (accurate to about one band), averaged over 15 October–1 November. Pixels are about 3.5 × 5.5 km, so peaks place a neighbourhood, not a road.
  • Dumps: Sentinel-2 shortwave infrared (20 m) on every clear pass since October 2024; Carbon Mapper methane records; BBC Urdu, 24 March 2025.
  • Kilns, plants and industry: SentinelKilnDB and the Brick Kiln Dataset for Pakistan's IGP Region; power plants and industrial areas from OpenStreetMap, as on HamariHawa's sources map.
  • Dust and weather: Copernicus CAMS global model via Open-Meteo (about 45 km grid); ERA5 reanalysis for wind.
  • Ground readings: PAQI's October analysis as reported by ProPakistani, and news reports of AQI on 1–2 November.
