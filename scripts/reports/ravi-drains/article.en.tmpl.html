The Ravi darkens below Lahore: a nine-year satellite baseline of river colour for checking measures on sewage and industrial discharge

By Hammad Tariq

Lahore's drains carry the city's sewage and the wastewater of its factories to the Ravi, largely untreated. The government has begun to act on both. A first large sewage treatment plant, at Babu Sabu, won federal approval in August 2026 at Rs{{treatment.babu_sabu_cost_billion_rupees}} billion, and the provincial environment department reports automatic water monitoring stations, two of them on the Ravi at Lahore, and squads to check what factories discharge. This report records the colour of the river up to {{dates.last_date|d}}, before construction of the Babu Sabu plant and before any results from the new monitoring programme have been published, so that the effect of these measures can be checked afterwards. We measured the colour of the water from satellite images on {{dates.measured}} selected low-cloud dates between {{dates.first_year}} and {{dates.last_year}}. On {{comparison.darker_below}} of the {{comparison.dates}} dates we could compare, the river was darker below the city than above it, by {{comparison.october_median_pct_darker}}% on a typical October date. The change begins at about the place where a dark channel from the Mehmood Booti side, most likely the Mehmood Booti drain, enters the river; that is a match in place, not proof of cause. Over nine Octobers the gap shows no clear trend. Colour is not a direct measure of pollution, and the images cannot tell sewage from factory waste; for that we quote studies that sampled the water. We will repeat the measurement as the measures take effect.

The river measurements are our own, made from the European Copernicus programme's Sentinel-2 images. They measure the colour of the water: how much red light it reflects. What is being checked

Dawn reported in October 2025 that Lahore "currently lacks a treatment facility" for wastewater and that the Ravi receives about {{treatment.untreated_mgd}} million gallons of it a day untreated. The water agency's environmental assessment for a sewer project, published by the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, puts untreated domestic sewage reaching the river at {{treatment.untreated_mgd_low}} million gallons a day. That is roughly {{treatment.untreated_million_litres_a_day_low|,}} to {{treatment.untreated_million_litres_a_day|,}} million litres. We have not measured it and give both figures. There are small plants: a February 2026 report said primary treatment plants were working at {{treatment.hudiara_societies_with_primary_treatment}} of {{treatment.hudiara_societies_registered}} registered housing societies along the Hudiara and at {{treatment.hudiara_industries_with_plants}} factories in one industrial cluster. None serves the city as a whole.

That is due to change. In August 2025 the provincial development working party approved a treatment plant at Babu Sabu, described as the city's first large one, at Rs{{treatment.babu_sabu_cost_billion_rupees_2025}} billion. On {{treatment.federal_approval|d}} the federal Executive Committee of the National Economic Council approved it at Rs{{treatment.babu_sabu_cost_billion_rupees}} billion, about {{treatment.french_agency_share_pct}}% of it from the French development agency. Dawn reported that this cleared the way for work to start within {{treatment.launch_within_months_of_approval}} months, once bidding is complete. The plant is to treat {{treatment.babu_sabu_phase_one_mgd}} million gallons a day in its first phase and {{treatment.babu_sabu_ultimate_mgd}} million eventually, with {{treatment.babu_sabu_preconstruction_months}} months of preparation and {{treatment.babu_sabu_construction_months}} months of building. In all, {{treatment.plants_planned}} plants are planned. Separately, in October 2025 the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank approved ${{treatment.aiib_million_usd}} million for three trunk sewers, about {{treatment.trunk_sewer_km}} km long together, meant to take sewage out of nearly {{treatment.drains_freed_km}} km of surface drains; the bank lists the Babu Sabu plant as an associated project paid for by the French agency. We found no report that construction of the plant has begun, and could not confirm it either way.

Sewage is one part of what the drains carry. The other is the wastewater of industry. By one report of May {{industry_sources.factories_identified_year}}, the provincial environment department told the Lahore High Court that it had identified {{industry_sources.factories_identified_2012}} factories discharging waste into the Ravi in the previous week and had sent them notices. That was not a settled count: its survey was still going on, and the court found that the department's list and the water agency's differed and ordered revised ones. A {{industry_sources.clusters_year}} paper from a federal water research institute counts {{industry_sources.units_in_clusters|,}} industrial units around the city, in {{industry_sources.clusters_large}} large clusters and {{industry_sources.clusters_small}} small ones.

Here too the government reports new measures. According to the Express Tribune of {{official_monitoring.reported|d}}, the provincial environment department has set up {{official_monitoring.stations}} automatic stations on rivers, canals and other water bodies across Punjab, each measuring {{official_monitoring.parameters}} properties of the water every hour. Of the places it names, {{official_monitoring.stations_named_on_the_ravi_at_lahore}} are on the Ravi at Lahore: near the Ravi Siphon, above the city, and where the Hudiara drain joins the river, below it. It has sent out "specialised water squads" to check industrial discharge, and is sampling the Hudiara drain at {{official_monitoring.hudiara_sampling_points}} points to work out how much of its pollution comes from local industry, how much from the city and how much from across the border. The same report says untreated sewage and contaminated water enter the river at about {{official_monitoring.entry_points_in_lahore}} places in Lahore, and that at least {{official_monitoring.treatment_plants_at_least}} treatment plants are to be built, where Dawn's earlier report said {{treatment.plants_planned}}. We have not seen data from the stations; the same paper reported on {{official_monitoring.data_not_public_reported|d}} that it was not open to the public. This record is meant as a check on all of these measures, on sewage and on industry.

The baseline: what the images show

Above Lahore the Ravi carries silt, fine soil held in the water, which makes it pale and bright. Below the city it is a darker brown. The two close-ups show the difference on one day, and the wider image shows where it happens: the river comes in from the top right pale and leaves at the bottom left dark. People who sampled the river from its banks have described the same thing. A {{ground.year}} study by an officer of the provincial irrigation department recorded the water as "yellowish" and "odorless" at the Ravi Siphon, above the city, and as "blackish" with an objectionable smell at Shahdara Bridge and at two places further down.

One image is one day. To see whether it holds, we went through every date on which the satellite catalogue lists an image of this stretch in two seasons, March to April and October, from {{dates.first_year}} to {{dates.last_year}}. There were {{dates.passes_in_the_two_seasons}} such candidate dates, usually {{dates.usual_days_between_acquisitions.0}} or {{dates.usual_days_between_acquisitions.1}} days apart, most of them cloudy. We kept the {{dates.chosen}} on which the catalogue recorded less than {{dates.cloud_cover_below_pct}}% cloud and the whole stretch was covered, and could measure {{dates.measured}} of them: {{dates.october}} in October and {{dates.spring}} in spring. Of those, {{shape.dates_in_the_profile}} had enough water above the city to set the upstream level, and they are the dates in the charts; {{comparison.dates}} had enough water in both of the stretches we compare. "Low-cloud" means under {{dates.cloud_cover_below_pct}}% cloud over the whole image tile, so a few images have some cloud in them. No date was added or dropped by eye, and the list of every candidate date, with the reason it was kept or left out, is in the evidence files. From November to February the haze is too thick, and in the monsoon the cloud. For every {{method.bin_m}} m of river we recorded how bright the water was in red light.

Each row in the chart is one year and each cell is 2 km of river, coloured by how bright the water was compared with the stretch above the city. Distances are counted downstream from the Ravi Siphon, where a canal passes under the river north-east of Lahore. Every October row has the same shape: pale down to the edge of the city, then darker. The spring rows show it too, with gaps where the river had shrunk to a channel too narrow to measure.

For a single figure, we compared two stretches on each date: the first {{method.upstream_km_below_siphon.1}} km below the siphon, which is above the city, and the stretch from {{method.downstream_km_below_siphon.0}} to {{method.downstream_km_below_siphon.1}} km, which runs past the south-west of the city and beyond. Both could be measured on {{comparison.dates}} dates, {{dates.october_with_both}} in October and {{dates.spring_with_both}} in spring, and the lower stretch was darker on {{comparison.darker_below}} of them. It was {{comparison.october_median_pct_darker}}% darker on a typical October date and {{comparison.spring_median_pct_darker}}% on a typical spring date. The two exceptions were {{comparison.exceptions.0.date|d}} and {{comparison.exceptions.1.date|d}}, when the two stretches were alike, within {{comparison.exceptions_within_pct}}%. The largest difference was {{comparison.most_pct_darker}}%, on {{comparison.most_date|d}}. These figures describe the colour of the water, not what is in it.

The fall begins near the city's northern side

Putting the October dates together, with each date counted once, gives the line in this chart. For about {{shape.upstream_flat_km}} km above the city the brightness does not change: the middle value stays between {{shape.upstream_flat_min}} and {{shape.upstream_flat_max}} of the upstream level. We take the fall to begin at the first kilometre from which the October middle value stays at least {{shape.onset_threshold_pct}}% below the upstream level for {{shape.onset_run_km}} kilometres running. That is about {{shape.onset_km_below_siphon}} km below the siphon, some {{shape.onset_km_above_old_ravi_bridge}} km above the Old Ravi Bridge. By {{shape.checkpoints.0.km_below_siphon}} km the water is typically {{shape.checkpoints.0.october_pct_darker}}% darker, by {{shape.checkpoints.1.km_below_siphon}} km {{shape.checkpoints.1.october_pct_darker}}%, by {{shape.checkpoints.2.km_below_siphon}} km {{shape.checkpoints.2.october_pct_darker}}% and by {{shape.checkpoints.3.km_below_siphon}} km {{shape.checkpoints.3.october_pct_darker}}%.

What is at that point? In the satellite images a thin black channel comes in from the east, from the Mehmood Booti side, and meets the river about {{junction.km_below_siphon_rounded}} km below the siphon, at about the same river kilometre as the October profile begins to fall. The picture below, taken on {{junction.image_date|d}}, shows the meeting place inside the white ring. From there the black water runs along the city-side bank before it mixes into the river. We read the meeting place from images with 10 m pixels, to roughly 100 m either way.

OpenStreetMap draws a drain on this line but stops it about {{junction.mapped_drain_ends_km_from_junction}} km short of the river, so our map shows no drain mouth here. The first mouth on the map is at {{mouths.first}} km, and the others are at {{mouths.second}}, {{mouths.third}}, {{mouths.fourth}} and {{mouths.last}} km. These are the black triangles on the charts; the white triangle is the dark channel from the Mehmood Booti side. The map does not name them or say what they carry.

We take the channel to be the Mehmood Booti drain, most likely, because a published study of that drain gives a sampling position that lies on the same visible channel. A study in the Pakistan Engineering Congress Journal in {{junction.study.published}} sampled the Mehmood Booti drain twice a day between {{junction.study.sampled_from|d}} and {{junction.study.sampled_to|d}}, at {{junction.study.sites}} places. One of them, the white square in the picture, is about {{junction.study.site_2_km_from_junction}} km from where the channel meets the river. The water had almost no dissolved oxygen, as little as {{junction.study.dissolved_oxygen_mg_l}} milligrams a litre. Its biochemical oxygen demand, a measure of organic waste, was {{junction.study.bod_mg_l.0}} to {{junction.study.bod_mg_l.1}} milligrams a litre, and it held {{junction.study.suspended_solids_mg_l.0}} to {{junction.study.suspended_solids_mg_l.1|,}} milligrams a litre of suspended solids. The authors describe the area as "densely industrialized" and the levels as far above the national standards. They did not measure metals.

So the fall in brightness begins at about the place where this channel reaches the river. That is a match in place. It does not establish that the drain causes the fall. The Mehmood Booti drain is the smallest of the eight drains in the published table below, about {{drain_flows.drains.0.pct_of_the_eight}}% of their published flow, and the fall keeps growing past the larger ones downstream. The images show where the change starts. They cannot show how much each drain adds.

The brown lines are every waterway that OpenStreetMap tags as a drain or a nala in this area, about {{drain_map.km_inside_the_map_frame}} km of them inside the frame. Not all of them reach the Ravi, some on the outskirts may carry farm or storm water, and the map is incomplete, mainly in the north of the city.

What the drains carry: sewage and factory waste

The drains do not carry sewage alone. A {{industry.year}} study of selected industries at the Quaid-e-Azam Industrial Estate reported about {{industry.industries}} industries there, sending an estimated {{industry.wastewater_cubic_metres_a_day|,}} cubic metres of wastewater a day to the Ravi through the Sattu Katla and Hudiara drains, with no treatment plant at the estate at the time. In the Mehmood Booti area, where the fall in brightness begins, OpenStreetMap marks factory chimneys beside the drain and a steel works nearby.

What this does to the river has been measured by sampling above and below the city, the same comparison we make from space. A {{industry_sources.sediment_year}} study in the journal Environmental Monitoring and Assessment took water and riverbed mud at the Ravi Siphon, above the city, and at three places at and below it. In the low-flow season the mud at Sundar, below the city, held more chromium than the mud at the siphon by {{industry_sources.sediment_pct_higher_below_city.chromium}}%, more cadmium by {{industry_sources.sediment_pct_higher_below_city.cadmium}}%, more nickel by {{industry_sources.sediment_pct_higher_below_city.nickel}}% and more lead by {{industry_sources.sediment_pct_higher_below_city.lead}}%. Its authors put this down to the city's domestic and industrial discharge. A {{industry_sources.pollution_index_year}} study in Environmental Sciences Europe classed {{industry_sources.pollution_index_pct_highly_contaminated.river_ravi}}% of its samples from the Ravi, and {{industry_sources.pollution_index_pct_highly_contaminated.drains}}% of its samples from the city's drains, as highly contaminated with metals.

A satellite cannot see metals. The brightness we measure can be changed by what is suspended and dissolved in the water in quantity, such as silt and organic matter; we have not measured which of these is at work. So this record follows the visible part of what the drains bring, and the sampling studies cover the part it cannot see. Judging whether the measures on industry are working will need both.

No clear trend in nine Octobers

If the gap between the river above and below Lahore were narrowing or widening, the yearly figures should drift one way. In October, as far as this record can tell, they do not.

What we will watch for

The measure we will follow is the October gap: how much less red light the river reflects below the city than above it. Its baseline is {{watch.baseline_pct}}%, and over {{dates.years}} years the yearly figure has stayed between {{watch.low_pct}}% and {{watch.high_pct}}%. The first phase of the plant would treat about {{treatment.phase_one_pct_of_untreated}}% to {{treatment.phase_one_pct_of_untreated_low_total}}% of the wastewater the river is reported to receive, depending on which total is used, so any early change is likely to be small, and one year will not settle it.

We set one rule in advance. If the yearly October figure falls outside the range of these nine years, below {{watch.low_pct}}% or above {{watch.high_pct}}%, for {{watch.years_running}} years running, we will report it. That is a trigger for reporting, not by itself evidence of improvement or of the opposite: a yearly figure rests on two to four dates and can move by chance, and the river's flow has to be taken into account. Water samples would be needed before saying what had changed. If the plant changes what reaches the river, the fall in the profile chart may also become shallower, first near the outfalls it serves; Dawn reports that the first phase will take sewage from the Cantonment Drain, Multan Road and Gulshan-e-Ravi pumping stations. The satellite's images are free, and we will add each new date that meets the same rule to this record as it comes.

Two things would make the check stronger. One is the place where the fall begins. The Mehmood Booti drain is not among the pumping stations named for the first phase of the Babu Sabu plant. A separate treatment plant for it, at Shadbagh, was put through an environmental assessment in {{official_monitoring.mahmood_booti_plant_assessment|t}}; we did not establish what has become of it. If the river stops darkening at {{junction.km_below_siphon_rounded}} km, that would be a reason to look at this drain. The other is the government's own stations near the siphon and at the Hudiara drain, which measure the water directly every hour. If their data is published we will set it beside this record.

What the satellite cannot tell

  • It sees colour, not pollution. Darker water is what dark sewage mixing into a silty river would look like, and the bank-side study saw the same change by eye. But brightness is not a count of bacteria, metals or oxygen. For those, water has to be sampled.
  • Other things can darken water. These are possibilities we have not measured. Silt may settle where a river slows. Deeper water over a dark bed looks darker, and through the city the river runs between embankments. Any clearer water joining the river would dilute the silt. Settling would be expected to be gradual, though, and what we see is {{shape.upstream_flat_km}} km with no change and then a fall that begins at about the place where a dark channel enters the river.
  • It cannot tell sewage from factory waste, or weigh one drain against another. The fall begins at about the place where the channel we take to be the Mehmood Booti drain reaches the river, and continues past the others. How much each drain adds, and how much of that is industrial, can only be settled by sampling at the outfalls.
  • It covers selected low-cloud dates in two seasons. There are no measurements from the monsoon, when the river is in flood, or from winter, when haze hides it. Of the {{dates.chosen}} dates we chose, {{dates.not_measured}} could not be measured because one of the two image tiles was not in the archive we read from, and on {{dates.measured_but_not_compared}} more one of the two stretches had too little wide water to compare.
  • Distances are approximate. They follow the river's centre line as drawn in OpenStreetMap, which is longer than today's channel in places.

We ran four checks on the measurement itself. First, the join between image tiles. The river crosses from one satellite image tile to the next {{control.join_km_below_siphon}} km below the siphon, almost exactly where the fall begins, and a mismatch between tiles could fake a step. The tiles overlap by about {{control.tile_overlap_km}} km, so we measured {{control.dates_checked}} dates again using the other tile across that stretch. The fall was {{control.dates.0.fall_pct_southern_tile}}% one way and {{control.dates.0.fall_pct_northern_tile}}% the other on the first date, {{control.dates.1.fall_pct_southern_tile}}% and {{control.dates.1.fall_pct_northern_tile}}% on the second, {{control.dates.2.fall_pct_southern_tile}}% and {{control.dates.2.fall_pct_northern_tile}}% on the third, and {{control.dates.3.fall_pct_southern_tile}}% and {{control.dates.3.fall_pct_northern_tile}}% on the fourth. The step is there using either tile on the four dates checked, which makes a mismatch between tiles an unlikely cause.

Second, side channels. The standard measurement takes water within {{mask_checks.standard.within_m_of_centre_line}} m of the river's mapped centre line, which can let in a side channel, a pond or a drain; the dark side channel near the start of the fall is about {{mask_checks.side_channel_m_from_centre_line}} m away. Measured again with only water within {{mask_checks.narrow_buffer_m}} m of the centre line, the river was darker below the city on {{mask_checks.within_300_m.darker_below}} of {{mask_checks.within_300_m.dates_compared}} dates, by {{mask_checks.within_300_m.october_median_pct_darker}}% on a typical October date and {{mask_checks.within_300_m.spring_median_pct_darker}}% in spring, and the fall began {{mask_checks.within_300_m.onset_km_below_siphon}} km below the siphon. Third, the banks. The width test looks along the east-west and north-south directions only, so some bank pixels can get through where the river runs diagonally. Using only pixels with water for {{mask_checks.core_m_from_any_edge}} m in every direction gave {{mask_checks.core_water_only.darker_below}} of {{mask_checks.core_water_only.dates_compared}} dates, {{mask_checks.core_water_only.october_median_pct_darker}}% in October and {{mask_checks.core_water_only.spring_median_pct_darker}}% in spring, with the fall beginning at {{mask_checks.core_water_only.onset_km_below_siphon}} km. Fourth, the choice of image. On {{mask_checks.scene_rule.dates_with_two_versions_of_a_scene}} dates the archive holds two versions of the same image, and our first run took whichever came first. We fixed a rule for which to use and measured every date again; those are the figures in this report. One date changed: {{mask_checks.scene_rule.gained_date|d}}, which the first run could not compare because it had read a clipped image, and which turned out to be one of the two dates with no difference. No other date's rounded figure changed; the largest change was {{mask_checks.scene_rule.largest_change_on_other_dates_points_exact}} of a percentage point.

Method and sources

Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance, tiles 43RDQ and 43SDR, read from Microsoft's Planetary Computer. Dates were chosen by a fixed rule from the Earth Search scene catalogue: both tiles present with less than {{dates.cloud_cover_below_pct}}% cloud and less than {{dates.no_data_below_pct}}% of the tile missing, 1 March to 20 April and 1 to 25 October. A pixel counts as water if it is brighter in green light than in shortwave infrared, dark in near infrared, not flagged as cloud or cloud shadow, and within {{mask_checks.standard.within_m_of_centre_line}} m of the river's centre line. Each water pixel is given the river kilometre of the nearest point on the centre line, and only pixels where the water is at least {{method.min_width_m}} m wide, both east to west and north to south, are kept, to cut down the bank pixels that get mixed in. The figure for each {{method.bin_m}} m of river is the middle value of the red band for those pixels. "Darker by {{comparison.october_median_pct_darker}}%" means the middle value for the lower stretch was {{comparison.october_median_pct_darker}}% below that for the upper stretch on the same date. In the charts each date counts once.
